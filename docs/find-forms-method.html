Find Forms: how the crawler works and how its keywords were chosen

A Selenium crawler that visits websites like a first-time visitor, finds their email newsletter signup forms, and records each one with screenshots and design measurements for manual coding.

1. What the crawler does

The study looks at how websites design their email subscription forms: footer signups, popups, slide-ins and sticky bars. For each site in a list, the crawler opens the site in a real Chromium browser, driven by Selenium, waits, scrolls and moves the mouse the way a visitor would, detects every signup form that appears, and saves what it found.

The detection logic is adapted from Englehardt, Han and Narayanan (2018), who built a crawler to sign up for newsletters at scale [1]. Our goal is different: we catalogue the forms instead of submitting them, so several parts of their method were changed (section 7).

Safeguards
  • Detection only. The crawler never types into, fills, submits or clicks anything. It moves between pages by opening link addresses directly.
  • Polite pacing. One browser visits one site at a time, with a 3-second pause plus 0–1 s of random jitter between page loads, and at most 3 pages per site by default.
  • Real browser only. Every request comes from ordinary Chromium page loads; nothing is fetched outside the browser.
  • robots.txt is not consulted. This was a deliberate project decision (8 Oct 2026) and should be stated in the methods section.

Larger lists can be split across up to 8 browsers running in separate processes (--jobs); their results are merged afterwards. A run can be stopped and resumed at any time: finished sites are logged and skipped on the next run.

2. How one site is crawled

  1. Fresh browser profile. Each site gets its own browser: a 1440 × 900 desktop window, US-English locale, no cookies or storage left over from earlier sites. Video and audio are not downloaded; images, fonts and third-party scripts load normally, because many signup forms are embedded from other services.
  2. Landing page. The homepage is scanned for forms (section 3).
  3. Follow links if needed. If the homepage has no signup form, the crawler ranks the visible links on the page by how likely they are to lead to one and visits the best ones in order, stopping as soon as a page yields a form, or after 3 pages in total.
  4. Time limit. If one site is still running after 6 minutes (2 minutes per allowed page), its browser is stopped, the site is logged as timed out, and the crawl moves on with a fresh browser. This guards against pages whose scripts never let the browser respond; in the pilot, two such sites had each held a browser for 30 minutes and almost 5 hours.

Link ranking

A link is checked against five groups in order and gets the first group it matches. Lower groups are visited first; links matching none are not visited. Links whose text mentions unsubscribe, mobile or phone, links whose address contains unsubscribe, and file downloads are always skipped.

3. How one page is scanned

Many signup forms are not on the page when it first loads: they open after a delay, after the visitor scrolls, or when the mouse moves toward the top of the window as if to leave (exit intent). The crawler therefore checks the page four times, once after each of these triggers, and records the first moment each form appeared.

Each check runs in every frame of the page, so forms inside embedded iframes are found as well.

4. How a form is recognised

Detection runs as a script inside the page. It uses two complementary approaches from the original paper: top-down, which starts from <form> elements, and bottom-up, which starts from an email box and looks for its button. The second catches the many modern forms built without a <form> tag.

Submit-like controls

Any <button> (except reset) and any submit or image input counts. A short clickable <div>, <span> or link counts only if its text, at most 40 characters, contains a submit word: subscribe, sign up, sign me up, join, submit, send, notify, register, get, continue, next, save, count me in, yes, go, ok, or the Spanish and Portuguese/French stems suscr… and inscr….

What kind of form is it?

Any form with an email box is recorded, but a good number are not subscription forms: a scan of the first 116 collected forms found roughly 26 login or account forms, 8 checkouts and 10 contact forms among them. Each form is therefore classified into a form_type, so those can be filtered out of analysis rather than quietly skewing it. The types are tried in order and the first match wins:

TypeRecognised by

A rule may also carry a blacklist that cancels a match, which is how a newsletter box reading “Already subscribed? Sign in” stays a subscription form, and how a footer offering both a newsletter and a way to get in touch is not read as a contact form.

The subscription category — the one this study is about — is recognised by its own rules rather than by elimination. That matters: a quote request, a registration or a gated download has an email box too, and under a leftover-bin definition each would have been counted as a subscription form. They now land in unknown, which is a manual-coding queue. Replaying the rules over the 166 forms collected so far gives about 105 subscription, 29 login, 8 contact, 4 search, 3 checkout and 17 unknown.

Two details of the login rules are worth recording. The page address counts because two-step logins show only an email box and “Continue”, with the password box on a screen the crawler never opens. And the text match requires a whole word, added after a pilot showed the looser version firing inside other words: 3 of the 5 forms it flagged in an 18-form pilot were newsletter forms caught by “By signing up, I agree…”.

Before 22 October 2026 this was a single yes/no column, login_like. The login rules are unchanged from it, so that category is comparable across the change; the other five types are new.

5. What is recorded

Each unique form on a site becomes one row (37 columns) in forms.csv, with a matching JSON line, an element screenshot, a screenshot of the whole window, and the form's HTML. Every page visit is logged in pages.csv, and every run in a timestamped run.log that ends with the total run time.

GroupColumns

Positionplacement_guess (fullscreen takeover, sticky bar top/bottom, modal popup, slide-in, footer, header, sidebar, in article, inline, near page bottom), z-index, popup hint, in iframe, in shadow DOM, width, height, share of the window covered
Fieldsnumber and types of fields, field names, checkboxes and how many are pre-checked, fields with no label

Columns such as placement_guess, form_type, modal_hint, consent_text_hint and heading are heuristics. They are starting points for manual coding and are checked against the screenshots.

6. How the keywords were collected

The paper's keyword lists decide two things: which links the crawler follows when the homepage has no form (section 2), and which custom buttons count as submit buttons (section 4). On 11 October 2026 we tested and extended these lists against real signup pages.

6.1 Data

We assembled a list of 146 URLs, from 144 sites, of pages known to contain an email subscription form (news sites, shops, Substack newsletters, company blogs and others). A separate helper script loaded each page in 5 parallel browsers, found the form with the crawler's own detection code, and recorded the page title, main heading, address path, and the form's heading, button text, email placeholder and surrounding text. As in the crawler, nothing was clicked or typed.

Form text. The text of the 108 forms was split into single words and two-word phrases and counted once per form: 3,091 terms, 363 of them used by 3 or more forms. Most frequent were field labels (first name, address) and legal text (privacy policy, terms). Terms describing the newsletter itself included news (25 forms), updates (15), weekly (14), latest (14), daily (12), and delivered, free, offers, events and stay (10 each).

Page addresses. 113 of the 146 URLs had a path (the rest were homepages). The most common path words were newsletter (40 pages), signup (27), subscribe (24), newsletters (20) and sign-up (8). The paper looks for signup words only in a link's text, never its address, so a link reading “Stay informed” that points to /newsletter was never followed.

Because a link keyword matches anywhere in a link's text, each candidate was judged by what other links it would also match, not only by how often it appears on signup forms.

6.5 Limits of the keyword study

  • The words come from the signup pages, while the link keywords are matched against the links pointing to them. The two usually share words, but not always.
  • The sample is 146 URLs and mostly English; the non-English additions rest on one or two forms each.
  • The effect on a crawl has not yet been measured. The planned test is to crawl a set of sites not in the 146 once with the old lists and once with the new ones, and compare how often a form is found and on which page.

7. Differences from the original method

Englehardt et al. [1] needed one form per site to submit; we need a complete catalogue of the forms a visitor sees. The main differences:

  • Every candidate form is recorded, not only the top-ranked one.
  • Bottom-up detection runs on every page, not only when top-down finds nothing.
  • Detection runs four times per page (load, delay, scroll, exit intent) so the trigger of each form is known.
  • Open shadow DOM is searched.
  • Links are opened by navigating to their address, not by clicking, and nothing is ever submitted.
  • The paper's “/2017” URL keyword is generalised to any year; the keyword lists were extended as described in section 6, including a new group that matches signup words in link addresses.
  • Login detection uses word boundaries, the text around the form, and the page address (section 4).
  • robots.txt is not consulted.
  • The crawler was moved from Playwright to Selenium on 21 October 2026. The measurement regime is unchanged: on the test fixture both produce identical rows for every form — placement, ranking, field types, text, widths and viewport coverage — provided the viewport is set exactly and scrollbars hidden. Two mechanics differ: the HTTP status is now read inside the page from PerformanceNavigationTiming, and audio and video are blocked by address rather than by resource type, so media served from an address with no file extension is now downloaded.

8. Limitations

  • Cookie banners can suppress or delay popups, especially on EU sites. Dismissing them would require clicking, which the method rules out.
  • Bot detection. Some sites serve different content to automated browsers. The browser window is visible by default for this reason.
  • Not detected: closed shadow DOM, forms drawn on a canvas, and forms behind CAPTCHAs.
  • Heuristic columns (placement, popup hint, form type, consent wording, heading) need checking against the screenshots.
  • Time-outs. A site stopped by the 6-minute limit keeps the forms found on its earlier pages, but none from the page it was stuck on.
  • Out of scope for the crawler: what happens after signing up (confirmation emails, double opt-in). Submitting forms is planned as separate work; see section 9.

9. Planned work: submitting the forms

The crawler described above never submits anything, and that will not change. Submission is planned as a separate tool, run deliberately on a chosen subsample, so that an ordinary crawl can never sign up for anything by accident.

Submitting would extend the study past the design of a form to what the form actually does: the confirmation flow, whether the site uses double opt-in, what the welcome email contains, and where the address ends up afterwards. Section 8 lists this as out of scope for the crawler; a dedicated tool is how it comes into scope.

Four questions need answers before any of it is written:

  • Ethics approval. Submitting creates real subscriptions, and on some sites real accounts, which detection-only crawling does not. Whether this needs IRB sign-off has to be settled first.
  • Address identity. One research address, or a per-site alias. An alias per site is what would make it possible to tell later which site sold or leaked an address, which is much of the reason to submit at all.
  • Pacing and scope. A submission is a write rather than a read: at most one per site, on a small subsample rather than the full list.
  • Records. What each attempt stores (fields filled, the response, the confirmation page) and whether it joins forms.csv or gets a file of its own.

The decisions above are recorded alongside the code so they are settled before implementation rather than during it.
